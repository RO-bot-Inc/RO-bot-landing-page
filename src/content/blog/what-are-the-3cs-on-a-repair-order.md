The 3Cs on a repair order are the **Complaint**, the **Cause**, and the **Correction**. Together they tell the story of a repair: what the customer reported, what the technician found, and what was done to fix it. Every OEM warranty program expects them, every auditor reads them, and every comeback investigation starts with them.

Most dealerships know the 3Cs exist. Far fewer write them well and consistently. This guide explains what belongs in each section, shows weak and strong examples side by side, and covers the habits that keep 3Cs complete on busy days.

## Why the 3Cs matter

The 3Cs are the legal and financial record of the work. They matter to three groups:

- **The manufacturer.** On warranty work, the 3Cs are how the OEM decides whether a repair was necessary, covered, and performed to procedure. A vague story is one of the most common reasons a claim is rejected or charged back after an audit.
- **The customer.** On customer-pay work, a clear story explains what they paid for and builds the trust that gets the next repair approved.
- **The dealership.** Good stories protect you in disputes, lemon-law cases, and comebacks, and they give the next technician a head start if the vehicle returns.

## Complaint: what the customer reported

The complaint is written by the service advisor at write-up, in the customer's terms, and verified by the technician. It should describe the symptom, not a diagnosis. Include when it happens, how often, and under what conditions.

**Weak:** "Check engine light."

**Strong:** "Customer states the check engine light came on two days ago and stays on. Engine runs rough at idle when cold, smooths out after about 10 minutes of driving."

The strong version gives the technician something to test and gives a warranty reviewer the context to judge the repair. If the technician cannot duplicate the concern, the story should say so and describe what was tried.

## Cause: what the technician found

The cause is the heart of the story and the part auditors read most closely. It should show the diagnostic path, not only the conclusion. A good cause section answers four questions:

1. Did you verify the concern?
2. What tests did you run, and with what tools?
3. What were the results, including codes, readings, and specifications?
4. What failed part or condition caused the concern?

**Weak:** "Found bad coil."

**Strong:** "Verified concern. Scanned and found P0302 (cylinder 2 misfire) stored. Misfire counter showed cylinder 2 misfires at cold idle. Swapped coils 2 and 3; misfire moved to cylinder 3. Inspected plug, no fouling or damage. Resistance on original cylinder 2 coil out of specification. Root cause: failed ignition coil, cylinder 2."

The swap test is what makes this story convincing. It proves the part caused the failure, which is what a warranty reviewer needs to see before approving a part replacement.

## Correction: what was done to fix it

The correction lists the repair performed and confirms the fix. Reference the labor operation, any TSB or service procedure followed, and the verification step.

**Weak:** "Replaced coil."

**Strong:** "Replaced ignition coil, cylinder 2, per service manual procedure. Cleared codes. Road tested 12 miles including cold-start idle; no misfires recorded, no codes returned. Repair verified."

## More 3C examples by repair type

### Brake noise (customer pay)

**Complaint:** Customer states a grinding noise from the front when braking, worse at low speed.

**Cause:** Verified noise on road test. Inspected front brakes: inner pads worn to backing plate on both sides, measured 0 mm. Rotors scored and measured below minimum thickness.

**Correction:** Replaced front pads and rotors, cleaned and lubricated slide pins, bedded pads. Road tested; noise eliminated.

### Inoperative power window (warranty)

**Complaint:** Customer states the driver window will not go up or down.

**Cause:** Verified concern. Switch input to door module confirmed with scan tool. Applied power and ground at motor connector; motor did not operate. Motor current draw zero. Root cause: open circuit internal to window motor.

**Correction:** Replaced driver window motor and regulator assembly. Performed window initialization procedure. Window operates through full travel, auto up/down functional.

### No-start (could not duplicate)

**Complaint:** Customer states the vehicle intermittently will not crank, three times in the past week.

**Cause:** Attempted to duplicate over 15 cold and warm starts; vehicle started each time. Battery tested good. No stored codes. Inspected starter and ground connections; all secure and clean. No TSB applies.

**Correction:** Could not duplicate concern at this time. Advised customer to return if the concern recurs and note conditions.

A "could not duplicate" story is still worth writing in full. It records diagnostic time and protects the store if the vehicle comes back.

## The most common 3C mistakes

- **Writing the cause as a conclusion only.** "Bad sensor" tells the auditor nothing about how you got there.
- **Missing measurements.** Readings and specifications turn an opinion into evidence.
- **Copy-paste stories.** Identical text across many ROs is a red flag for warranty auditors.
- **No verification step.** The correction should say how you confirmed the repair worked.
- **Complaint and cause that don't match.** If the customer reported a noise and the cause describes a leak, the claim will be questioned.

## How to get better 3Cs from every technician

Most weak stories have the same cause: technicians are paid to fix cars, and typing is slow, so the write-up gets squeezed to a few words at the end of the job. A few changes help:

- Give techs a short template for the cause section (verify, test, result, root cause).
- Have the shop foreman review warranty stories before they go to the warranty administrator.
- Let technicians speak their findings instead of typing them. Voice capture removes the main reason stories come out short.

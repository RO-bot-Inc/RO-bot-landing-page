## How do you calculate warranty parts markup?

Divide total eligible parts charges by total eligible parts cost, subtract 1, and multiply by 100, unless your statute says otherwise. [Minnesota](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/80E.041), [California](https://california.public.law/codes/vehicle_code_section_3065.2) and [Ohio](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-4517.52) all spell out this method.

Using the example above, $18,000 ÷ $10,000 = 1.8. Subtract 1 and multiply by 100 to get 80%.

## Is parts markup the same as parts margin?

No. Markup is profit as a percentage of cost. Gross margin is profit as a percentage of the sale. Your parts manager may quote margin, so convert before you compare it with a markup filing.

| Cost | Sold for | Markup | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| $100 | $140 | 40% | 28.6% |
| $100 | $160 | 60% | 37.5% |
| $100 | $180 | 80% | 44.4% |

## Why does the averaging method matter?

The same ROs can produce very different results under different methods. Take a $10 part sold for $20 (100% markup) and a $1,000 part sold for $1,400 (40% markup). Total charges over total cost gives 40.6%, because the expensive part carries more weight. A simple average of the two percentages gives 70%.

[Florida](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0300-0399/0320/Sections/0320.696.html) is an exception to the formula above. If you and the manufacturer do not reach agreement within 30 days of your written request, parts compensation is the greatest of three things: your arithmetical mean percentage markup on the sample, the manufacturer's highest suggested retail or list price, or a markup based on your parts gross profit percentage from your financial statements.

## What repair orders go in the sample?

A common sample is 100 sequential customer-pay ROs or 90 days of ROs, whichever is less, from the last 180 days. [Minnesota](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/80E.041), [Illinois](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081507100K6.htm), [Nebraska](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=60-1438) and [Tennessee](https://codes.findlaw.com/tn/title-55-motor-and-other-vehicles/tn-code-sect-55-17-121/) all use a version of it. Florida uses 50 consecutive retail repairs from the three months before your request. It also requires 10 days' written notice, and the manufacturer can pick the starting RO in that window.

Then remove only what your statute excludes. Minnesota excludes promotional repairs, wholesale parts, routine maintenance, fasteners without a part number, tires, reconditioning and accessories.

Big-ticket assemblies are where states split. Florida excludes engine and transmission assemblies. Minnesota excludes them only if the dealer agrees to a handling charge instead of a markup.

Expect to go through a lot of ROs. Frank O'Brien wrote in [a 2018 F&I article](https://www.fi-magazine.com/articles/how-to-get-retail-rate-for-warranty-parts) that reaching 100 qualifying ROs could take more than 1,000 actual ones, and California requires you to include the nonqualified ROs from the same period.

## Can a parts markup filing lower your rate?

Yes. In [Beck Chevrolet v. General Motors](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/litigation-mediation-arbitration/new-york-dmv-appeals-board-affirms-decision-rejecting-excessive-warranty-reimbursement-rate-demanded-by-dealer), a New York dealer went from 73% to 62.30% after two qualifying transmission repairs were included.

The dealer had declared 81.62%. GM found the two repairs had been left out, added them back and cut the rate. New York's DMV Appeals Board upheld GM in October 2019. This account comes from an article by GM's counsel in the case, so it is one side's summary, under one state's law.

*Note: the dealer also proposed averaging the markup per RO, which produced 85.18%. That method lost too.*

Before you submit, have a second reviewer check every skipped RO and every excluded parts line against the statute.

## What are the warranty parts markup rules by state?

They vary on how often you can file, how long the manufacturer has to respond, and when the rate starts. These six show the range. All were checked on November 8, 2026. Laws change, so confirm yours with your state dealer association or counsel before you file.

## Are you getting paid on zero-cost parts?

Pull a few recent OEM-supplied exchange claims and compare what the OEM paid with the reimbursement method in your state. A handling allowance may be less than your statute calls for.

The rules differ. [Minnesota](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/80E.041), [Nebraska](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=60-1438), [Montana](https://mca.legmt.gov/bills/mca/title_0610/chapter_0040/part_0020/section_0130/0610-0040-0020-0130.html) and [California](https://california.public.law/codes/vehicle_code_section_3065.2) tie payment to your markup and a defined wholesale value of the part. [Florida](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0300-0399/0320/Sections/0320.696.html) uses its parts compensation method, less the dealer cost in the price schedule. [Illinois](https://www.ilga.gov/documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/081507100K6.htm) pays your markup on most supplied parts, but caps entire engine, transmission and electric vehicle battery assemblies at up to 30% of what you would have paid for the assembly.

The calculator above starts with this Montana example, from [a 2024 article by Jordan Jankowski](https://montana-auto-dealer.thenewslinkgroup.org/?p=5131) of warranty-recovery firm Armatus: $995 × 80% = $796, or $771 above the $25 handling allowance in that example.

Armatus sells this service, so test it against your own statute. Jankowski says many manufacturers make dealers ask for this payment, and he advises checking how the claim is characterized so it does not affect your warranty expense report.

## Who does what before you file?

Hand the work off by role. The [worksheet above](#ro-sample-worksheet) has a tab for each of these roles.

**Fixed ops director**
- Confirm the current OEM markup in writing.
- Size the opportunity with your own warranty parts cost.
- Own the filing calendar: earliest filing date, OEM response deadline and effective date.

**Service and parts managers**
- Pull the RO sequence your statute requires, without hand-picking.
- Reconcile parts charges and parts costs against the ROs.
- Document skipped ROs separately from excluded parts lines, with the reason for each.

**Service advisor**
- Before closeout, check that the customer concern, technician finding and repair performed line up with the parts on the RO.
